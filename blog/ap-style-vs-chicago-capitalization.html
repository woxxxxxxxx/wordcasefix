AP vs Chicago Title Capitalization: The Current Rules

By WordCaseFix | Updated July 29, 2026 | 8 min read
The short answer AP capitalizes prepositions of four or more letters in title case. Chicago's eighteenth edition capitalizes prepositions longer than four letters. That means AP writes With and From, while Chicago writes with and from. Both capitalize a longer preposition such as Between.

AP and Chicago agree on the core principle: capitalize the first and last words and the major words in a title. The visible differences come from how each guide handles prepositions, hyphenated compounds, and the choice between title case and sentence case.

Why older charts conflict: Chicago changed this rule in the eighteenth edition. Chicago 17 lowercased prepositions regardless of length. Chicago 18 capitalizes prepositions longer than four letters. If a chart says Chicago lowercases every preposition, it is describing the previous edition.

Hyphenated compounds

Hyphenated words need more judgment than a simple length rule. AP generally capitalizes each principal part. Chicago considers the role of each element, so short articles, prepositions, and conjunctions inside a compound can remain lowercase.

When a publication has a house style for compounds, use that instruction first. Automated converters are useful drafts, but they cannot always determine whether a word is acting as a preposition, adverb, or part of a proper name.

Title case or sentence case?

AP style does not require every newsroom or organization to display every headline in title case. Many publishers use sentence case for headlines: capitalize the first word and proper nouns, then lowercase the remaining words. Chicago's title-capitalization rules are commonly used for books, chapters, articles, and other named works.

Which style should you use?

Choose AP

Use AP when you write for a newsroom, press office, public-relations team, or organization whose house guide names AP.

Choose Chicago

Use Chicago for book publishing, long-form editorial work, and organizations whose house guide follows the Chicago Manual.

If no style is required, choose one system and document the choice. Consistency across the same publication matters more than switching rules from title to title.

Job titles and geographic terms

Both systems distinguish a formal title attached to a name from a generic job description. For example, President Maya Chen uses a formal title before a name, while Maya Chen, the president uses a descriptive title. Both also lowercase compass directions used for movement and capitalize recognized regions, such as the Midwest.

Frequently asked questions

Is "with" capitalized in AP style?

Yes in AP title case because with has four letters. A publisher using sentence case would still lowercase it unless it begins the title.

Is "with" capitalized in Chicago style?

No. Chicago 18 capitalizes prepositions longer than four letters, so the four-letter word with remains lowercase unless it begins or ends the title.

Did Chicago change its capitalization rules?

Yes. The eighteenth edition changed the treatment of longer prepositions. Chicago 17 lowercased prepositions regardless of length; Chicago 18 capitalizes prepositions of five letters or more.
